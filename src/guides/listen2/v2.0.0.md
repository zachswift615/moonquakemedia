## 1. Getting Started

For years, your options when considering how best to listen to text read aloud on iOS have relied on the built-in system voices or cloud-based services that charge a monthly fee, send your documents to someone else's servers, and often restrict usage. Listen2 takes a different approach. It runs multiple voice engines entirely on your device — from high-fidelity neural voices to Apple's own system voices — all without an account, cloud processing, or tracking. Your documents stay on your phone and never leave.

In one sentence: Listen2 turns your documents into personal audiobooks using on-device text-to-speech.

Getting from zero to listening takes about 60 seconds. A voice comes pre-installed, so there is no need to download or configure anything first. The process is as follows:

1. **Import something** — tap the <span class="button-label">+</span> button and select a PDF, EPUB, text file, Word document, or DAISY book from your device or cloud service. You can also import text from your clipboard.
2. **Press play.**
3. **Listen.**

And because everything runs on device, there is no signup screen, no email verification, no "create your account to continue." You open the app and you are in. No account needed.

#### Privacy

Your documents are yours. Nothing gets uploaded, analyzed, or shared. The text-to-speech engine, the voices, the processing — it all happens locally on your iPhone or iPad. Whether you are importing work emails, personal journals, or other content, that content stays private by default. Personal content remains personal. No setting adjustments necessary.

#### Trial and Licensing

Listen2 comes with a 7-day free trial so you can make sure it fits your workflow. After that, it is a one-time purchase. Not a subscription. You buy it once and it is yours for life.

## 2. Importing Content

You have text scattered everywhere. PDFs from work, articles you saved in Safari, ebooks you downloaded, notes you copied from an email. Listen2 can pull content from all of these places. Here are all the methods for getting content into your library.

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "file-text", "inline-icon" %} Supported File Types</h4>
<ul>
<li><strong>PDF</strong> — the universal document format. Text-based PDFs work best.</li>
<li><strong>EPUB</strong> — the standard ebook format. If you buy DRM-free books, this is what you get.</li>
<li><strong>DOCX</strong> — Word documents from Microsoft Office or Google Docs exports.</li>
<li><strong>TXT</strong> — plain text files. Simple and reliable.</li>
<li><strong>Markdown (.md)</strong> — formatting gets stripped for reading aloud, but the text comes through clean.</li>
<li><strong>DAISY</strong> — an accessibility-focused format used by many libraries and organizations.</li>
</ul>
</div>

#### Import from Files

Tap the <span class="button-label">+</span> button and choose "Import from Files." This opens the standard iOS Files picker, which means you are not limited to what is on your device. iCloud Drive, Dropbox, Google Drive, OneDrive — if it shows up in your Files app, you can import from it. Pick your file, and it lands in your library.

#### Import from Clipboard

This one is more useful than it sounds. Say you just asked ChatGPT a question while you are out on a walk and you want to hear the answer instead. Tap the copy button at the bottom of ChatGPT's message, switch to Listen2, tap <span class="button-label">+</span>, choose "Import from Clipboard," and you are done. Works with anything you can copy — web articles, emails, notes, recipes, and more.

#### Import from Link

Tap <span class="button-label">+</span>, choose "Import from Link," and paste in a URL. Listen2 fetches the page, strips out the navigation bars and ads, and keeps the article text. You get the content without the clutter. Google Drive share links work here too — if someone shares a document with you via a Drive link, paste it in and Listen2 will grab the file. The Google Drive link needs to have "anyone with link" can view permissions. Google Drive files with "restricted" sharing access will not work.

#### Using the Share Extension

This is the most convenient option for web browsing. When you are in Safari or any other app, you see something you want to listen to later, and you tap the Share button. Choose Listen2 from the share sheet. Listen2 extracts the article and shows you a preview — the title, site name, and full text — so you can confirm the content looks right before saving. Tap Save, and the article gets added to your library. If a page does not extract well (some pages rely heavily on JavaScript), you will see an error with the option to try again or cancel.

When you switch to Listen2 after saving, the app picks up the new article immediately and shows a brief loading indicator while it finishes importing. No need to close and reopen the app.

And if you import a batch of articles throughout the day this way, you can group them into a collection in two taps — see Web Article Collections in the Collections section.

#### Using Listen2 with Siri

If you have text on your clipboard and want to skip the UI entirely, just say "Read my clipboard in Listen2." It imports the clipboard text and starts playing automatically. Hands-free from start to finish.

#### Collection Archives

If someone sends you a `.listen2collection` file — a curated reading list, a course packet, or a set of documents organized into chapters — just open it. The complete collection imports with all its documents and structure intact. Tap the file, and everything lands in your library ready to go.

#### PDF Extraction

Importing a PDF used to mean staring at a spinner until the app finished. That is over. The progress bar now advances per page as Listen2 reads each one. For a 300-page book, you see it climb steadily instead of guessing whether the app is stuck. If you decide you do not want to wait, there is a **Cancel** button right on the import overlay. Tap it and the import stops cleanly — no half-imported document sitting in your library.

The standard PDF import method is not quite "standard." It was built from scratch to handle multi-column research papers, technical books, and just about any PDF you can throw at it. It extracts the text layer, filters out footnotes and other noise, and re-joins hyphenated line breaks that disrupt voice reader prosody.

That same cleanup also removes bracket citation references like `[1]`, `[24, 55, 127]`, or `[1-5]` from the body text. These are meant for visual skimming. Read aloud, they interrupt the flow of every other sentence in a research paper.

But citation brackets carry information. If you listen to academic work and you want those references spoken, open the main Settings screen, find the **PDF Import** section, and turn off **Filter Citation Brackets**. Imports after that will keep the brackets intact. This setting only affects the Standard extraction path. AI Vision and OCR handle citations separately.

On iOS 26 or later, you also have a choice of extraction methods from the import picker. Standard is the default and works for most documents. **AI Vision** uses on-device layout analysis to figure out reading order in tricky PDFs — multi-column academic papers, unusual formatting, text wrapped around figures. And as of v1.9.0, iOS 26+ users can pick **Vision OCR** directly from the same menu. That one is worth reaching for when you know a PDF has a text layer but the text is garbled — unusual encodings, broken fonts, or documents that read as gibberish even though the text copies fine. Vision OCR re-reads the document as an image and usually gets it right.

If a page or two of the PDF cannot be read — damaged, unsupported content, corrupt fonts — the import still finishes. You see a brief toast like "Imported, but 3 pages could not be read" and the document lands in your library. You decide whether to keep it or delete it and try a different extraction method.

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "wand-2", "inline-icon" %} Scanned-PDF Detection and OCR Fallback</h4>
<p>Some PDFs are not really PDFs. They are images of pages dressed up in a PDF wrapper — scanned books, photographed documents, or "searchable PDFs" where the underlying text layer is OCR garbage. In older versions of Listen2, these imports either failed with a generic error or produced unreadable text that you had to hunt through to figure out what went wrong.</p>
<p>Listen2 now detects scanned and image-only PDFs automatically. On a large document, the app flags the problem within the first 10 percent of pages and stops extraction early so you are not waiting five minutes for a failed import. On a short document, the check runs at the end. Either way, you see a dialog offering to switch to OCR.</p>
<p>On iOS 26 or later, the OCR path uses Apple's new document recognition engine. It preserves layout — tables read as tables, multi-column pages read in the right order. On iOS 18.2 through 25, Listen2 falls back to an earlier OCR engine. That version reads top to bottom and does not handle multi-column layouts well. The dialog warns you about the limitation before you confirm.</p>
<p>The dialog always offers a way out. For the early-exit variant, you can **Try OCR** or **Cancel**. For the final check on smaller documents, you can **Finish as-is**, **Try OCR**, or **Cancel**. If the first pages looked sparse but the rest of the document extracted cleanly, "Finish as-is" is the right call.</p>
</div>

<div class="warning-box">
<h4>{% icon "alert-triangle", "inline-icon" %} Password-Protected PDFs</h4>
<p>Password-protected PDFs cannot be imported. Listen2 detects the encryption up front and shows a clear error — "This PDF is password-protected and cannot be imported." — instead of hanging or failing partway through. Remove the password in another app first, then try again.</p>
</div>

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "eye", "inline-icon" %} PDF Import with VoiceOver</h4>
<p>The import menu is a standard action sheet, so VoiceOver reads each option clearly as you swipe through them. The file picker is the system Files app picker — fully accessible with the same navigation you are used to everywhere else on iOS.</p>
<p>During PDF import, VoiceOver announces the progress message as it updates — for example, "Extracting page 42 of 300." The Cancel button on the progress overlay is a standard button with a clear label.</p>
<p>The scanned-PDF detection dialogs are standard system alerts. VoiceOver reads the full prompt, including the layout-limitations warning on iOS 18.2 through 25, before you reach the action buttons. Each button is labeled with the action it performs — "Try OCR," "Keep current text," "Finish as-is," or "Cancel" — so there is no ambiguity about what confirming will do.</p>
<p>The partial-extraction toast (for example, "Imported, but 3 pages could not be read") posts an accessibility announcement so VoiceOver speaks it as soon as the import finishes, even if the toast itself is brief.</p>
</div>

## 3. Your Library

Once you start importing documents, you will need to be able to find them again. That is the Library tab — the first thing you see when you open Listen2.

Think of your library as a stack of everything you have brought in, sorted by what you touched most recently. Whatever you were listening to last is sitting right at the top. You do not organize it. You do not file things into folders. It just stays out of your way and keeps the thing you care about most front and center.

Each row in the list shows you what you need at a glance: the document title, a format icon or cover thumbnail, your reading progress as a percentage if you have started it, and how long ago you last opened it. You can tell the difference between "that PDF I was halfway through yesterday" and "that EPUB I have not touched in two weeks" without opening either one.

When your library gets big enough that scrolling feels tedious, there is a search bar at the top. Type the first few characters of a title and the list filters down instantly.

You will also notice a second tab at the top — Collections. That is for curated playlists of documents, and it is covered in detail in Section 11. For now, just know it is there.

#### Sorting Your Library

By default, documents are sorted by most recently read. But you can change that. Tap the sort button in the toolbar (the up-and-down arrows icon) to open the sort sheet. Six options are available:

- **Recent** — most recently read documents first. This is the default.
- **Title (A to Z)** — alphabetical.
- **Title (Z to A)** — reverse alphabetical.
- **Newest First** — most recently imported documents at the top.
- **Oldest First** — first imported documents at the top.
- **Type** — grouped by document format (PDF, EPUB, Text, and so on), then alphabetical within each group.

Your sort preference is remembered across sessions. Set it once and it sticks.

#### Choosing a Layout

A list is great for scanning titles. But if most of your library is books with covers, a list hides the best part. Sometimes you just want to see your shelf.

Tap the layout button in the toolbar, right next to the sort button. Three layouts are available:

- **List** — one document per row, with a small cover and the details beside it. This is the default.
- **Grid** — two large covers across on iPhone, with the title and your progress underneath.
- **Compact Grid** — three smaller covers across on iPhone, so you see more of your library at once.

On an iPad or a Mac, the grids fit as many covers across as the window allows, so a wide screen does not blow each cover up to poster size.

Documents without a cover, like a pasted article or a text file, still get a proper tile. Listen2 shows the format icon and the title on a colored card instead of a blank box. And a thin bar along the bottom of each cover shows how far you are into it.

Like your sort order, your layout is remembered. The Collections tab has its own sort and layout settings, covered in Section 11.

#### Managing Documents

To manage your documents, press and hold on a document to bring up a context menu — from there you can **rename** the document, **add it to a collection**, **export the plain text**, or **delete** it. In the list layout, swiping left on a row is a faster way to delete. The grid layouts do not have swipe, so use the press-and-hold menu there.

On a Mac there is no swipe. Right-click the document instead (or Control-click, or two-finger click on a trackpad) and the same menu appears, Delete included. The same goes for every other list in the app: anything you can swipe to delete on iPhone, you can right-click to delete on a Mac.

Renaming is useful when the imported title does not match what you want to see. Long-press the document, tap Rename, type the new title, and confirm. The original file is untouched — only the display title in your library changes.

#### Exporting the Plain Text

Listen2 does a lot of work to turn whatever you imported — a complex PDF, an EPUB, a web article — into clean, readable text. That extracted text is useful outside the app too. Maybe you want to send a chapter to a friend, paste an article into your notes, or keep a backup copy in another tool.

Long-press a document and tap **Export Text**. Listen2 writes the plain text to a `.txt` file named after the document and opens the standard share sheet. From there you can save to Files, AirDrop to another device, mail it to yourself, or hand it off to any app that accepts text. The export contains the same text Listen2 narrates, with paragraphs separated by blank lines.

This is the cleanest way to get a plain-text version of a PDF or EPUB without copying and pasting page by page.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Automatic Position Saving</h4>
<p>Listen2 saves your position automatically whenever you pause or leave a document. Come back three days later, open it up, and you are right where you stopped. That said, there are times when auto position saving does not work — for example, if the app crashes before saving or if your phone dies during playback. Using bookmarks liberally is a good safety net.</p>
</div>

But here is the thing that matters most: Listen2 remembers where you are in every document, not just the last one. You can jump between five different books over the course of a week and each one picks up exactly where you left off. It is the kind of thing you stop noticing because it just works.

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "eye", "inline-icon" %} Your Library with VoiceOver</h4>
<p>Navigate the document list by swiping left and right. Each row announces the document title, reading progress, and time since last read. Delete is available as a custom action — swipe up or down on a row to find it. Rename, Add to Collection, and Export Text are also available as accessibility actions on each row. ## 4. The Reader

This is the screen you will spend the most time with. Once you open a document and press play, the reader is where everything happens — following along, navigating, adjusting speed, jumping between chapters. It is worth knowing what every button does and the few gestures that make a real difference.

#### Top Bar

Starting from the left, you have five buttons that control navigation and discovery.

The **library icon** returns you to the Library. The **bookmark button** drops a bookmark at your current position, or removes one if there is already a bookmark there. **Search** lets you find text within the document. **Table of Contents** shows chapters, sections, and bookmarks so you can jump straight to the part you care about. And the **Display Settings button** (the Aa icon) opens a compact sheet for text appearance — highlighting, auto-scroll, font, spacing, and column width.

#### The Text

The text area is not just a display — it is interactive.

**Tap any paragraph** and playback jumps to the start of that paragraph. Useful when you want to skip ahead a couple of paragraphs or rewind without scrubbing through a progress bar.

**Double-tap any specific word** and playback seeks to exactly that word. Handy when you want to re-hear a tricky passage or start from a specific spot mid-sentence.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Double-Tap to Seek</h4>
<p>Double-tap any word and playback jumps right to it. Handy when you want to re-hear a tricky passage or start from a specific spot. It works anywhere in the document — even paragraphs the voice has not reached yet.</p>
</div>

Between these two gestures — tap for paragraph, double-tap for word — you can get to any point in a document in about a second. Much faster than dragging a scrubber.

On a Mac, a double-click selects a word the way it does in any other text on your Mac. So the Mac gets a menu instead. Right-click any word (or Control-click it) and choose **Play from Here**. Playback starts at that word. The same option appears on iPhone and iPad when you press and hold a word.

#### Bottom Bar

The bottom bar handles playback controls and settings access, laid out in two rows.

The top row has the **Audio button** (speaker icon) on the left, a **seekable progress bar** in the center, and the **Navigate button** on the right. Below the progress bar sit elapsed time, a jump-to-percentage button, and remaining time — all updating every second during playback. The bottom row has the **skip back** and **skip forward** buttons, which jump between paragraphs, and the **play/pause button** in the center where your thumb expects it. When a timer is active, the countdown replaces the play button area.

**Audio Settings** (speaker icon) opens a sheet with everything about how the voice sounds: playback speed, voice selection, the Voice Profile, paragraph pause, sleep timer, figure narration, pronunciation rules, and audio cues. This is where most of your adjustments happen.

**Navigate** opens a compact sheet with position controls: **Jump to Playback Position** snaps the view back to wherever the voice is currently reading, **Jump Back** returns you to wherever you were before your last jump or seek (like a browser back button), and a **Skip Level** picker lets you choose whether the skip buttons jump by paragraph, sentence, or chapter.

#### Collection Context

When you open a document as part of a collection, the reader adds a bit of context at the top — the collection name and your position, something like "Morning Reading - 2 of 5." Extra skip buttons also appear so you can jump forward or backward between documents in the collection without going back to the library. Everything else works the same.

## 5. Playback

Once you have imported a document and opened it in the reader, it is time to control it. The good news: the controls you will reach for most are extremely simple, and they work even when you are not looking at the screen.

#### Play and Pause

The big button at the bottom of the reader. Tap it and the voice starts. Tap it again and it stops. But you do not have to be in the app to do this. Lock your screen, switch to another app, pull up Control Center — play and pause work from all of them. Same goes for your AirPods or any headphone remote. One tap to toggle.

#### Skipping Forward and Back

The skip buttons sit on either side of the play button. By default, they jump by paragraph, which is the right move for most reading. But if you want finer control, you can switch to sentence-level skipping in settings. These also work from the lock screen and headphone remote, so you can rewind a paragraph without pulling your phone out of your pocket.

#### Speed

Tap the **Audio button** (speaker icon) in the bottom bar to open Audio Settings, where speed is the first control. You can go from 0.5x all the way up to 2.5x, in fine increments. Start at 1.0x. Then bump it up a notch every few sessions. Most people land somewhere around 1.2x to 1.5x for casual reading. For dense or technical material, 1.0x is the right call. There is no badge of honor for listening fast — the goal is comprehension. Note that Kokoro voices are capped at 1.4x — the model needs to keep up with real-time synthesis, so faster speeds are not available for that engine.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Adjust Speed Gradually</h4>
<p>Start at 1.0× and bump it up 0.1× at a time. You will be surprised how quickly 1.3× starts to feel natural. Your brain adapts faster than you would expect, and you can always dial it back for harder material.</p>
</div>

#### Paragraph Pause

This one is subtle, but it matters. There is a configurable gap between paragraphs — from 0.0 seconds up to 1.0 seconds. It gives you a beat to process what you just heard before the next paragraph starts. Think of it like the white space on a printed page. You will find it in settings.

#### Duration and Progress

Listen2 estimates how long your document will take to read based on the active voice engine and your current playback speed. The bottom bar shows a seekable progress bar with elapsed time on the left and remaining time on the right, both in H:MM:SS format. A percentage button sits between them — tap it to type a specific percentage and jump straight there.

The duration starts as an estimate and gets more accurate as you listen. Listen2 measures the actual speed of the voice engine during synthesis and refines the estimate in real time. By a few minutes in, the numbers are close to exact.

The progress bar is fully seekable. Drag it to jump anywhere in the document. This works for long books just as well as short articles — drag to the 40% mark of a 300-page PDF and playback resumes from that spot.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Quick Navigation</h4>
<p>For precise jumps, tap the percentage button between the time labels and type a number. For example, type "50" to jump to the halfway point. Faster than dragging when you know exactly where you want to go.</p>
</div>

#### Timer

Set a timer and Listen2 pauses playback when it expires. Tap the **Audio button** (speaker icon) in the bottom bar and scroll to the Sleep Timer section. You will find four preset durations — 15, 30, 45, and 60 minutes — plus a custom field where you can type any value from 1 to 120 minutes. Use the plus and minus buttons to adjust in 5-minute increments.

An optional audio cue toggle plays a gentle tone when the timer finishes, so you know time is up even if you were not watching. Tap Start and the countdown begins. The remaining time appears in the bottom bar next to the play button, replacing the normal controls while the timer is active. Tap the countdown to cancel it at any time.

This is not just for falling asleep. Set a 30-minute timer when you want to cap your listening session — whether it is knowing when to start cooking dinner, catching your bus stop, or wrapping up a study block.

#### Background Playback and Now Playing

Switch apps, lock your screen, put your phone in your bag. The voice keeps going. Your lock screen shows the document title, current section or chapter name, cover artwork if the document has it, and a progress scrubber that you can drag to seek. Here is a small change you may notice. Pause for a moment and press play, and the voice carries on from the exact word where it stopped. #### When Your Phone Is Locked *(new in v1.13)*

There is one limit you should know about. While your phone is locked, iOS caps how much processing any app can use. Every voice in Listen2 creates its audio on your device as it reads, and some voices work much harder than others. Phonon and Supertonic use only a small share of what iOS allows, so they read through a locked phone for hours without coming close. Kokoro voices are different. They use most of the allowance, and on a warm phone they can need more than iOS permits.

In the past, iOS handled that by shutting Listen2 down. The reading simply stopped, with no explanation. Now Listen2 watches its own processing while your phone is locked and slows its preparation to stay inside the limit. If it still cannot keep up, it pauses at the end of a sentence, so you never lose your place mid-word. A short spoken notice in your voice's language tells you what happened: "Listen2 paused because iOS limits processing while your phone is locked. Unlock your phone to keep listening."

So unlock your phone. iOS sets no such limit while the screen is on, and Listen2 picks up at the next sentence as soon as you press play. You can also press play from the lock screen or your AirPods. That works too, but with a Kokoro voice on a warm phone, it may pause again after a few minutes. And when you next open the reader, a short banner explains the pause. Tap the X to dismiss it, or press play and it goes away on its own.

## 6. Figure Descriptions

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} v1.5.0 Feature</h4>
<p>Figure descriptions are an upcoming feature in Listen2 v1.5.0. Currently available for PDF and EPUB documents only. If the feature proves useful, support will expand to web articles and other document types in future updates.</p>
</div>

Books, textbooks, and technical documents are full of images — charts, diagrams, photographs, illustrations. When you read visually, you glance at these and keep going. When you listen, those images are invisible. Figure descriptions fix that. Listen2 uses an on-device vision model to examine each image in your document, generate a natural-language description, and read it aloud at exactly the right moment during playback.

Everything happens on your device. No images are uploaded anywhere.

#### How It Works

When you import a PDF or EPUB that contains images, Listen2 asks whether you want to describe them. You choose a detail level — Brief for a single sentence per image, or Standard for a longer description with more visual detail — and tap Describe. The app downloads a one-time vision model (approximately 350 MB), then processes each image on-device. For a typical book with 20 images, the whole thing takes under a minute.

Once descriptions are generated, they are stored alongside your document. You do not need to regenerate them unless you want a different detail level.

#### During Playback

When the voice reaches a paragraph that contains an image, three things happen in quick succession. Playback pauses momentarily. A short audio cue and haptic tap signal that a figure description is coming. Then a description sheet slides up with the generated text, and the voice reads it aloud in the same voice you are already listening to.

The description sheet is a full mini reader — it shows the text with sentence highlighting and auto-scroll, just like the main reader. When the description finishes, the sheet auto-dismisses after a short pause and playback resumes from exactly where it left off. If a paragraph has multiple images, each one gets its own sheet in sequence.

You do not need to do anything. But if you want to interact with the description, you have full control:

- **Play and pause** the description narration independently
- **Skip between sentences** within the description using the transport controls at the bottom
- **View the original image** by tapping the photo icon in the top right — this opens a full-screen viewer with pinch-to-zoom
- **Dismiss early** by tapping Done or swiping the sheet down — playback resumes immediately

#### Viewing the Original Image

The figure description sheet includes a View Image button in the toolbar. Tap it to see the original image from your document in a full-screen viewer. You can pinch to zoom in and out, double-tap to toggle between fit-to-screen and zoomed views, and tap the close button to return to the description. The voice keeps reading while you look at the image, so you can follow along visually and audibly at the same time.

#### Image First *(new in v1.13)*

Here is the situation this solves. You are following an exercise book, a cookbook, or an illustrated manual, and the picture matters more to you than the words describing it. Opening the image by hand at every figure gets old fast.

So you can choose what is on screen while a description is read. In Audio Settings, under Figures, set **Show During Descriptions** to **Image**. From then on, each figure opens straight to the full-screen picture while the description is read aloud. The description still plays. Only the view changes.

A bar at the top of the image gives you three controls. **Done** closes the figure and goes back to your book. The play button pauses or resumes the description. **Text** switches to the highlighted description text, and the narration keeps going. From the text, the photo icon brings you back to the image. Whichever you choose, the other one is always a tap away.

If a figure has no image to show, Listen2 simply shows the description text instead.

#### Non-English Documents

If your document is not in English, Listen2 detects the language and translates the generated descriptions to match. The translation happens automatically using Apple's on-device Translation framework, right after the vision model finishes its work. If the translation language pair is not installed on your device, the app asks whether you want to download it or continue with English descriptions instead.

#### Settings

You can manage figure descriptions from the Audio Settings sheet in the reader (the speaker icon in the bottom bar). Here you will find:

- **Describe Figures / Re-describe Figures** — run or re-run the description generation. If your document already has descriptions, the button shows "Re-describe" along with the image count. You can also choose to describe only images from your current reading position forward.
- **Read Figure Descriptions** toggle — turn narration on or off without deleting the descriptions themselves. When off, playback skips over figure descriptions entirely.
- **Show During Descriptions** — choose **Text** (the default) to see the description text highlighted as it is read, or **Image** to see the figure itself. Either way, the description is read aloud, and you can switch to the other view at any time.
- **Detail Level** — choose between Brief (one sentence) and Standard (a few sentences with more visual context). You can re-describe at a different level any time.

If you do not want to be prompted about figure descriptions at all, open the main Settings screen and turn off **Offer to Describe Figures**. With this off, the app will not ask whether you want to generate descriptions after importing a document. You can still generate them manually from Audio Settings in the reader whenever you choose.

## 7. Highlighting & Auto-Scroll

Reading along while someone talks is surprisingly hard if the words are not visually tracking with the voice. Your eyes lose the spot, you glance away for a second, and suddenly you are scanning the page trying to find where the audio is. Highlighting and auto-scroll solve this together — one shows you which words are being spoken, the other makes sure those words stay on screen.

#### Highlighting

You have three levels to choose from: **Word**, **Sentence**, and **Off**.

**Word** is the default, and it is the most precise. Each word lights up the moment it is spoken. Listen2 runs a speech alignment engine entirely on your device to figure out exactly when each word starts and ends. If you do not pay attention to the word highlighting, you may get better battery performance having it set to "sentence" or "off".

So what does the highlight look like? In light mode, the spoken word sits on a soft yellow rectangle. In dark mode, the rectangle is a calm blue instead, because bright yellow behind every word is a lot of glare when you are reading at night. Either way, the text itself never changes color. Only the rectangle moves, gliding from one word to the next, so nothing on the page blinks while you read.

**Sentence** highlights the full sentence being read. Less visual movement, but it still gives you a clear anchor for where the voice is.

**Off** turns highlighting off entirely. You are just listening.

There is also a **line band** option — a subtle highlight strip behind the current line. It is only available with Word-level highlighting, and you toggle it independently in settings. Think of it as a reading ruler. If you are following along closely, it gives your eyes one more thing to lock onto.

Note for older devices: on older devices with less available memory, Listen2 automatically caps highlighting at Sentence level to keep things running smoothly. If that applies to your device, you will see a note in settings explaining why Word is not available.

The same automatic step-down applies to third-party system voices such as CerePlay and ReadSpeaker. These voices do not report word timing, so Listen2 uses sentence highlighting while one is active — even if your setting is Word. See the Voices & Languages section for more on third-party voices.

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "sparkles", "inline-icon" %} When to Use Each Highlighting Level</h4>
<p><strong>Word:</strong> Best for close following, language learning, or reading along carefully. <strong>Sentence:</strong> Good balance of visual tracking without too much movement. <strong>Off:</strong> When you are just listening and not looking at the screen.</p>
</div>

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Dial It Down</h4>
<p>If word-level highlighting feels too busy, try sentence highlighting. It still gives you a visual anchor without the per-word movement.</p>
</div>

#### Auto-Scroll

Two modes here: **Page-follow** and **Centered**.

**Page-follow** is the default. You read down the page naturally, and when the highlighted line gets close to the bottom of the screen, the view scrolls forward to keep it visible. It is like reading a book where someone turns the page for you right when you need it.

**Centered** works differently. It pins the currently spoken line to the middle of the screen. The text flows past you like a teleprompter — your eyes stay in one spot, and the words come to you. Great for study, close reading, or language learning where you want every word front and center.

But here is the important detail. Centered mode requires Word-level highlighting to work. It needs that precise per-word timing to know exactly which line to pin. If you switch highlighting to Sentence or Off, centered mode automatically falls back to Page-follow and shows a brief toast message letting you know what happened. No crash, no confusion — it just gracefully steps down and tells you why.

So when should you use which? Page-follow is the one for casual listening — you glance at the screen now and then, perhaps you are cooking or commuting and checking in occasionally. Centered is for when the screen has your full attention and you want to lock in. Two different modes for two different situations.

Both settings live in Display Settings, accessible from the **Display Settings button** in the reader's top bar. You can switch between them mid-session without interrupting playback.

The Listen2 engine comes in two versions of the same model. Both read with the same voices. One is built into the app. The other is a separate download. Listen2 chooses the right one for your device on its own, based on how much memory the device has, so most people never need to think about it.

Before a voice can speak, the text has to be turned into sounds, and Listen2 used to borrow an outside open-source tool for that step. It now uses its own pronunciation models, one for each of the 13 languages, built into the app. Several Piper voices have also been retrained on that new pronunciation. There are a few things to know about Kokoro. Because the model is large, playback speed is capped at 1.4x to keep up with real-time synthesis. Kokoro requires a device with at least 6 GB of RAM — on older devices that do not meet this threshold, Kokoro voices will not appear in the Voice Library. And because the model runs a larger neural network on device, your phone will produce more heat and use more energy during extended playback compared to Piper. **Supertonic** is the high-fidelity engine. It uses a flow-matching neural architecture that produces more natural, expressive speech. Supertonic voices sound closer to human narration — smoother prosody, more natural rhythm, clearer articulation. Supertonic supports all 13 of Listen2's languages. Voices carry an orange badge.

Phonon brings that same class of natural, expressive narration to every device Listen2 runs on. It is a compact neural model from Gradium that runs on Apple's Neural Engine, and it reads English with smooth phrasing and steady pacing, even through long chapters. Phonon voices carry a teal badge.

Phonon has 25 English voices, with American and British accents, female and male. **All 25 come built into the app**, so there is nothing to download. Hadley is the default voice. Switching between Phonon voices is instant, and Phonon starts speaking in under a second. Because Phonon is part of the app, its voices show a "Bundled" label in the Voice Library and cannot be deleted.

A few things to know. Phonon speaks English only. Playback speed works across the full range, from 0.5x to 2x. And word highlighting uses a timing model trained on Phonon's own voices, which also comes built in.

**System** voices are Apple's built-in speech synthesis voices — the same ones available across iOS. They require no download within Listen2 because they are already on your device (though higher-quality tiers may need to be downloaded from iOS Settings first). System voices are available in every language Apple supports and come in several quality tiers:

- **Premium** and **Enhanced** — the highest quality system voices. These sound significantly better than the defaults and are shown in Listen2 by default.
- **Compact**, **Basic**, **Eloquence**, **Novelty**, and **Standard** — lower-quality tiers that are hidden by default but can be revealed in the voice filter settings if you want to browse them.

System voices are the best option when battery life and energy efficiency are your priority. Because they use Apple's native speech synthesizer directly — no neural network inference, no model loading — they have the lowest energy impact of any engine in Listen2. Even lighter than Piper. If you are listening for hours and want to stretch your charge, or if your device runs warm with the neural engines, Premium or Enhanced system voices are the way to go.

Here is the important part: Premium and Enhanced voices are not installed on your device by default. You need to download them from iOS Settings before they will show up in Listen2. The process is straightforward but easy to miss if you do not know where to look.

1. Open the **Settings** app on your iPhone or iPad.
2. Go to **Accessibility**, then **Spoken Content** (on iOS 26 and later, this is called **Read & Speak**).
3. Tap **Voices**, then select your language (for example, **English**).
4. You will see a list of voice names. Each one has a quality label underneath it — look for voices marked **Premium** or **Enhanced**. These are the ones worth downloading. Premium voices are the highest quality Apple offers, and Enhanced voices are a step below but still sound very good.
5. Tap a Premium or Enhanced voice and tap **Download**. The download size varies but is typically a few hundred megabytes per voice.

Once a voice finishes downloading in iOS Settings, it appears in Listen2 automatically — no restart needed. If you skip this step and open Listen2's voice library with the System engine filter on, you will only see the lower-quality voices that come pre-installed on every device. The default voices work fine, but the Enhanced and Premium voices offer a far superior listening experience.

**Third-party voices** *(improved in v1.11.3)*. Some apps on the App Store — CerePlay and ReadSpeaker among them — install their own text-to-speech voices onto your device. Once installed, these voices work anywhere Apple's system voices work. And that includes Listen 2.

Getting them to appear takes two steps. First, open the third-party app and download a voice inside it — if the voice does not register with iOS right away, restarting your device usually sorts it out. Second, in Listen 2's Voice Library, open the Sort & Filter sheet and turn on the **Standard** quality tier. Third-party voices appear under Standard, which is hidden by default. They show "On Device" like other system voices — there is nothing to download inside Listen 2.

Two behaviors differ from Apple's own voices, and both come down to the same cause: third-party voices do not report word timing to the app. So Listen 2 automatically uses sentence highlighting while one is active, even if your highlight setting is Word. And when you pause, the voice stops completely rather than holding its place — pressing play again restarts the current sentence from its beginning.

All six engines work side by side. You can switch between Listen2, Piper, Kokoro, Supertonic, Phonon, and System voices freely, even mid-document. Word-level highlighting works with all six engines, with one exception: third-party system voices fall back to sentence highlighting, as described above. The app handles everything behind the scenes. Only one engine is loaded at a time, so switching between engines tears down the old one before starting the new one.

#### Downloading & Managing

Tap the download button next to any voice and you will see it move through three phases: downloading, decompressing, and installing. Most voices run 60 to 75 MB each, so Wi-Fi is highly recommended.

To delete a voice, swipe left on it in the Voice Library, or press and hold it (right-click on a Mac) and choose Delete. The built-in Listen2 and Phonon voices cannot be removed. They are your safety net. Voices you made yourself can be deleted like any download (see <a href="#create-voice">Create a Listen2 Voice</a>).

A few deletion rules are worth understanding before you start deleting. Piper voices are individual downloads, so each one deletes on its own. Supertonic and Kokoro work differently: every voice in those families shares one downloaded engine, and the engine is where nearly all the disk space lives — each individual voice adds only a small amount on top. That means deleting any Supertonic voice removes the Supertonic engine and every Supertonic voice with it. Same for Kokoro. But nothing is lost for good: download any voice from that family later and the rest come back with it.

One more rule: you cannot delete the voice you are currently using. And because Supertonic and Kokoro voices share an engine, you cannot delete any voice from those families while one of them is active. Switch to a different voice first, then delete.

System voices are the exception. They belong to iOS, not to Listen 2, so the app cannot remove them. To manage system voices, open the Settings app and go to Accessibility, then Spoken Content, then Voices.

At the bottom of the library screen there is a storage info section that tells you how many voices you have installed and the total disk space they are using. Handy when you are cleaning house.

#### The Book Chooses the Language *(new in v1.13.1)*

Here is the problem this solves. You finish an English novel, open a German one, and Luna starts reading German words with an English accent. Before, you had to remember that Supertonic had a language setting and go find it. Now you do not.

It does this on your device, from a few pages spread through the book, so a title page or a copyright notice in another language does not fool it. There is one more shortcut. In the Voice Library, filter to a language and then choose a Supertonic voice. Listen2 takes the hint and reads in that language.

#### Language Packs

Non-English voices can optionally download a grammar pack for their language. These adjust parameters such as text normalization — turning things like "1,234" into "one thousand two hundred thirty-four" instead of reading out individual digits. Same for dates, currency, and other formatted text.

If a voice needs a grammar pack, you will see a prompt. Download it, and numbers and dates will sound natural. You can manage these from the Language Packs screen — download, delete, or retry if something went wrong. They are small and make a real difference in how polished the output sounds.

One thing to know: grammar pack coverage varies by language. Most handle numbers, dates, and currency well. Vietnamese normalizes numbers and basic text correctly, but currency symbols (like ₫ or $) and measurement units (km, kg, kWh) currently pass through as-is. The voice will still read the text — it just will not expand "100000 ₫" into words the way it would for other languages. This is on our list to improve.

Every voice engine has its own handful of settings, and in earlier versions they lived in different places under different names. In 2.0 they come together in one section called **Voice Profile**. It always shows the settings for the voice you are using right now, and nothing else. Switch to a voice from another engine, and the Voice Profile changes to match.

**Listen2 Voices: Pace and Liveliness**

Here is a situation you may know. A voice sounds right for a novel, but it rushes through a contract. Or it reads the news with more drama than the news deserves. The playback speed slider helps with the first problem, but it does so by stretching the finished audio. A Listen2 voice can do better, because the model itself can choose how fast to speak and how much expression to put in.

**Pace** sets how fast the voice speaks on its own. It has seven steps, from slowest to fastest: **Slowest**, **Slower**, **A little slower**, **Even**, **A little faster**, **Faster**, and **Fastest**. The voice stays the same at every step. Only its pace changes. Your playback speed still applies on top, so you can pick a calm pace and still listen at 1.3x.

**Liveliness** sets how much the voice rises and falls. It has five steps: **Flattest**, **Calm**, **Moderate**, **Lively**, and **Most lively**. Lower is calmer and more even. Higher is more animated.

The new Listen2 voices start at a Pace of **Slower** and a Liveliness of **Most lively**. That starting point gives each word room and keeps the reading expressive. A few voices start somewhere else, because it suits them better. You can also choose **Natural** for either setting: tap **Natural** beside it, and the voice chooses its own pace and liveliness for each sentence, the way a person reading aloud would.

These settings belong to each voice. Slow down one voice for careful reading, and your other Listen2 voices keep their own settings. Voices you make yourself have a Voice Profile too. Changes take effect at the next sentence, so you can adjust them while you listen and hear the difference right away.

**Piper Voices**

Two sliders shape how Piper voices sound.

**Expressiveness** (0.00 to 1.00) controls pitch and tone variation. Set it low and the voice goes flat, almost monotone. Set it high and you get more animated, dynamic delivery.

**Duration Variation** (0.00 to 1.00) controls how long each sound is held. Low means even, mechanical pacing. High means more natural rhythm with subtle pauses and rushes.

Each Piper voice now starts from its own defaults *(new in v2.0)*. The older voices start at 0.667 and 0.80. The retrained 1.5 voices start at 0.50 and 0.33, which is what they were trained to sound best at.

**Supertonic Voices**

Five sliders let you dial in Supertonic's output.

**Voice Tempo** (1.00 to 1.50) controls the model's natural speaking pace. Higher values give faster, more energetic speech straight from the model, without stretching the audio. Default is 1.05.

**Articulation Speed** (0.70 to 1.30) controls how much time the model allocates per word. Lower values give the model more room to pronounce clearly. Words come out more precise, but the natural pace slows down. Higher values speed up articulation but may clip syllables. Default is 0.79.

**Duration Buffer** (0% to 30%) adds extra time after the model's predicted speech length. This prevents words from being cut off at the end of sentences. If you hear the last word getting clipped, bump this up. Default is 14%.

**Quality Steps** (10 to 40) controls how many cleanup passes the model runs on the audio. More steps produce cleaner, more natural audio but take longer to generate. Default is 12, which is a good balance for the current Supertonic model. Raise it if the audio sounds rough.

**Variation** (0.50 to 1.50) controls how much randomness shapes the audio. Lower values sound smoother and more consistent. Higher values introduce more expressiveness but can also cause the voice to shift accents between sentences. Default is 0.85.

#### Voice Presets *(new in v1.5.0)*

Here is the problem presets solve. You find the perfect tuning for Luna — she sounds crisp and clear at Articulation Speed 0.90 with Quality Steps 28. But then you switch to Atlas for a different book, and those settings come along for the ride. Atlas sounds better with completely different values. Without presets, you are manually adjusting sliders every time you switch voices.

Presets let you save a set of tuning parameters and load them onto any voice. Each voice can have its own settings, independent of every other voice. Change Luna's Articulation Speed and Atlas does not budge.

**How it works.** By default, all voices share one set of global tuning parameters. When you save or load a preset onto a voice, that voice *detaches* from the global settings and gets its own copy. The section header tells you the current state — "Global" means the voice is using shared settings, a preset name like "Crisp Narration" means it loaded that preset, and "Custom" means it has its own settings that do not match any preset.

**Saving a preset.** Open the Presets screen from the Voice Profile (there is a "Presets" link below "Reset to Defaults"). Tap "Save Current as Preset." The app suggests a name based on the voice — accept it or type your own. Saving captures the current slider values and also detaches the voice, so future slider changes only affect this voice.

**Loading a preset.** On the Presets screen, tap any saved preset to load it onto the current voice. The sliders update immediately. For Piper voices, you get the same "Apply Now" or "Apply Soon" choice you see when changing sliders.

**Using Global Settings.** If a voice is detached and you want it to go back to sharing settings with everyone else, tap "Use Global Settings" on the Presets screen. The voice-specific settings are removed and the sliders revert to the global values.

**Reset to Defaults** still works the same way, with one nuance. If the voice is detached, it resets *that voice's* settings to factory defaults but keeps it detached. The voice does not rejoin the global pool — it just gets fresh default values of its own. If the voice is using global settings, it resets the global values, which affects all voices that have not been detached.

Listen2 voices work a little differently *(new in v2.0)*. Their Pace and Liveliness always belong to each voice, so there is no global setting for them to share or return to. Presets still work for them: save the Pace and Liveliness you like on one Listen2 voice, and load that preset onto another.

Presets are filtered by engine. Listen2 presets only appear when a Listen2 voice is active, Supertonic presets only appear when a Supertonic voice is active, and Piper presets only appear for Piper voices. Deleting a preset from the library does not affect any voice that already loaded it — the loaded values are a snapshot, not a live link.

#### Where to Start

Open the **Voice Library**. The first thing at the top is a card called **Create a Listen2 Voice**. Tap **Create a voice**. You can also start from the reader: open the voice picker, scroll to the bottom, and tap **Create a Listen2 Voice**.

The first screen lists what you need:

- **A quiet room.** The voice copies the room it hears.
- **About two minutes.** Twenty seconds of reading, then one sentence.
- **The person whose voice it is.** They read both parts themselves.

Then you choose how to start: **Record a voice** or **Choose an audio file**.

#### Recording a Voice

Recording takes four short steps. Each one checks something that would otherwise spoil the voice, so the twenty seconds of reading are rarely wasted.

**1. Choose a microphone.** Listen2 lists three kinds of microphone: the **iPhone microphone**, a **Wired headset** such as EarPods, and an **Audio interface** (a USB box that a studio microphone plugs into). The ones that are not connected are dimmed. Plug one in and Listen2 selects it for you. If your interface has more than one input, choose which one to record from. Below the list, an input level meter asks you to say a few words at your reading volume. It reads **Too quiet**, **Good** or **Too loud**. Aim for Good. The meter is advice, not a gate, so you can always continue.

Bluetooth microphones, such as AirPods, are not on the list in this version. Use the iPhone microphone, a wired headset or an audio interface instead.

<!-- TODO(confirm): on a Mac, the design falls back to a single row for the Mac's current input, with a pointer to System Settings, under Sound (create-voice design section 9, recommendation 4, not yet confirmed). -->

**2. Listen to the room.** Stay quiet for three seconds while Listen2 listens. The voice will copy whatever it hears, so a fan or a fridge in the background ends up in every pause the voice makes. You get one of three answers: **Quiet enough**, **A little noisy** or too noisy. If the room is too noisy, Listen2 explains why and suggests a few fixes. You can check the room again, or record in this room anyway.

**3. Read aloud.** Listen2 shows a short passage. Tap the round record button and read it at your own pace, the way you would read to someone. After ten seconds, a tag says **Enough recorded**. Finish your sentence, then tap stop. Recording stops by itself at thirty seconds. If you would rather not read the passage, tap **Speak in your own words instead** and talk about your day, or describe the room you are in, for twenty seconds. Any steady speech works.

**4. Say the consent sentence.** This step is explained below. It is short, and it matters.

Then Listen2 makes the voice. It finds the clearest five seconds of your reading, matches its volume to the other voices, builds the voice, and reads a first sample. That takes a few seconds.

#### The Consent Step

A voice is personal. So before Listen2 makes one, the person who read the passage says one sentence aloud, and Listen2 checks that both recordings are the same person. The screen is titled **A voice belongs to its speaker**. You will see a sentence like this one:

> "This is my voice, and I agree to Listen2 using it to read aloud on this iPhone. Lantern, orchard, seven."

Tap **Record the sentence** and say it. The three words at the end change every time, so an old recording cannot stand in for the speaker. Listen2 checks two things, on your iPhone: that it heard the three words, and that the voice saying the sentence is the same voice that read the passage.

The first time you reach this step, Listen2 asks for permission to use Speech Recognition. It uses it only to hear the three words, and only on your iPhone. If your iPhone cannot recognize speech on the device, Listen2 checks the voice match alone.

If the two do not match, Listen2 tells you so and does not make the voice. There is no way to skip the check. A mismatch is often caused by something simple, so try again with these in mind:

- Say the sentence from the same place, at the same distance.
- Use the same microphone for both recordings.
- The person who read the passage must be the one who says the sentence.

Your reading is kept while you try again, so you only need to repeat the sentence. And if it keeps failing, start over and record both parts in one sitting. That often helps.

Tap **Why Listen2 asks for this** on the consent screen for the short version: Listen2 makes a voice only when the person it belongs to has said, aloud, that they agree. The check runs on your iPhone, and the recordings stay there. No check can catch every imitation, so please make voices only of people who have agreed.

Listen2 keeps the consent recording with the voice, on your iPhone only, together with the date and the three words. It is never uploaded or synced, and it is deleted when you delete the voice.

#### Making a Voice from an Audio File

Maybe you already have a good recording: a podcast clip of yourself, or a voice memo from a quiet room. Choose **Choose an audio file** on the first screen and pick the file. WAV, M4A and MP3 recordings work. Listen2 uses the clearest five seconds of speech in it, so a recording with one speaker and little background noise works best.

The consent step still applies, and the speaker must be there to say the sentence live. Listen2 asks **Whose voice is in this file?** Choose **It is my voice**, or **It is someone who is with me** and pass them the iPhone. Tap **Continue to the sentence**. The speaker then says the consent sentence, and Listen2 checks that it is the same person as in the file. If the speaker cannot say the sentence, Listen2 cannot make the voice. That keeps every voice with the person it belongs to.

#### Your Voice Is Ready

The last screen plays your new voice. Tap **Hear a sample** to hear eight seconds of it, or use **Try it on a paragraph** to hear it read a paragraph with the word highlight following along. Tap **Use your own text** to paste in something of your own. Give the voice a name; it starts as "My Voice". Then tap **Save voice**. Listen2 saves it, selects it, and you are ready to read.

Not happy with it? Tap **Record again**. Your microphone and room results are kept, so you go straight back to the reading.

If anything about the recording is worth a second look, such as long pauses, a clipped word or a noisy background, it is listed under **Worth checking** on this screen. The voice is still made. The note just tells you what to fix if you record again.

#### Using Your Voices

Your voices appear in the Voice Library under **Your Listen2 voices**, right below the card, and at the top of the reader's voice picker under **Your voices**. Each one has a play button for its sample, just like the built-in voices. Tap a name to read with that voice. They work like any other Listen2 voice: same word highlighting, same Voice Profile with Pace and Liveliness.

Swipe left on one of your voices for three actions: **Rename**, **Record again** and **Delete**. Recording again replaces the voice in place, so documents that use it keep using it.

## 10. Pronunciation

Sometimes a voice says a word wrong. A character name, a technical term, a word with two readings. You hear it wrong once, and it pulls you out of the listening every time after that. Listen2 has two tools for this. The first one works on its own. The second one is yours.

#### Reading Words by Their Meaning *(new in v2.0)*

English has words that are spelled the same but said two ways. You **read** a book today, and you **read** one yesterday. A **lead** pipe, and the dog on a **lead**. A **tear** in your eye, and a **tear** in your shirt. A reading voice has to guess, and a wrong guess makes you stop and replay the sentence in your head.

Listen2 voices now read these words by their meaning. Before a sentence is spoken, Listen2 looks at the whole sentence and works out which reading it needs. Then it tells the voice exactly how to say the word. This happens on your iPhone, for words such as read, lead, tear, record, used and Polish. When Listen2 is not sure, it leaves the word to the voice rather than force a wrong reading.

Accents settle it too. "Résumé" with its accents is always the noun, the one you send with a job application, and the same goes for words such as exposé and saké. Without the accents, "resume" is read by its meaning like any other word.

This is on by default and works with Listen2 voices. To turn it off, open **Settings** and find **Read Words by Their Meaning** in the **Pronunciation** section. With it off, the voice decides every time.

#### Pronunciation Rules

Reading by meaning fixes the common words. For everything else there are pronunciation rules. You tell the app "when you see this text, say it this way." The displayed text stays exactly the same. Only the spoken output changes.

Open pronunciation rules from the Audio Settings sheet (speaker icon in the bottom bar) while reading, or from the main settings screen under Voice. Tap <span class="button-label">+</span> to create a rule. You set a match pattern (the text the voice gets wrong) and choose whether it should match the exact word or any text containing the pattern. Then, under **Say It As**, choose one of three kinds of rule:

- **Sounds Like** *(new in v2.0)*. Write how the word sounds. This is the most exact kind, and it is the one the form starts on.
- **Replace**. Read other text instead, such as a spelling the voice handles better.
- **Delete**. Stay silent and skip the matched text entirely.

#### Sounds Like Rules *(new in v2.0)*

Here is the problem with spelling a word differently to fix it. You are still asking the voice to guess, just from a different spelling. Sounds Like rules skip the guessing. You write the word the way it sounds, the way a newspaper or a dictionary spells out a name, and a Listen2 voice says exactly those sounds.

Writing one takes three rules:

1. **Break the word into parts, with hyphens between them.** Saoirse becomes SEER-shuh. Nike becomes NY-kee.
2. **Write the loudest part in capitals.** That is the part you stress: nuh-WIN for Nguyen. A word with one part needs no capitals: red, win.
3. **Use spaces between words.** MAR-ee AN.

Not sure which letters make a sound? Tap **Sound Key** in the form. It lists what to type for every vowel and consonant, each with example words. A few things it explains: "uh" and "er" sound full in the loud part (cup, bird) and light everywhere else (about, butter). A "y" before a vowel is the y in yes, and anywhere else it is the vowel in my. Always write g for the g in go, and j for the j in jam.

As you type, the form shows the sounds it understood, each with a key word, for example "s (sun) · EER (near) · sh (she) · uh (about)". If a letter is not in the key, or a part has no vowel, the form says so. Then type a sentence into **Preview text** and tap **Preview** to hear the rule with your current voice.

Sounds Like rules work with every voice, in two ways. A Listen2 voice says the exact sounds you wrote. Every other voice reads a plain-word version of your spelling instead, and the form shows it under the sounds: "Other voices read: ..." So the rule still helps when you switch to another engine. It is just less exact there.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} When a Sounds Like Rule Does Not Land</h4>
<p>Most sounds-like spellings come out right the first time. Unusual names and made-up words are the hardest. If a word still sounds off, check the sounds line in the form against the Sound Key, try moving the capitals to a different part, and preview again.</p>
</div>

#### Other Rules That Come in Handy

- **Spelling out abbreviations.** For something like "NASA," use a Replace rule with periods between letters: "N.A.S.A." This tells the voice to spell it out letter by letter. Spaces between letters do not work as well.
- **Silencing footnotes.** The app ships with built-in rules for common footnote markers like [1], [2], and reference symbols like † and §. Toggle them on in the rule list and those markers disappear from the audio without touching the display text.

Rules can be **global** (apply everywhere) or **document-scoped** (apply only to a specific document). When you open pronunciation rules from the reader's Audio Settings, you can create rules scoped to whatever you are currently reading. Document-scoped rules take priority over global ones when both match the same text.

You can toggle rules on and off, reorder them by dragging, and delete any rule you have created. Swipe left on it, or press and hold it (right-click on a Mac) and choose Delete. Built-in rules can be toggled but not edited or deleted.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Writing Good Replace Rules</h4>
<p>A Replace rule hands the voice ordinary text, so write the replacement as natural words the voice can pronounce, such as "Kahtuh" for Kaethe. Capitals and hyphens only mean something in a Sounds Like rule. In a Replace rule, "KAH-tuh" may be read letter by letter. For abbreviations, use periods (N.A.S.A.) not spaces (N A S A).</p>
</div>

## 11. Collections

You know the feeling of making a great mixtape. You pick the tracks, put them in the right order, add a little commentary between songs to set the mood. Collections are that, but for reading. You grab a set of documents from your library, arrange them in the order you want, and hit play. Everything plays through front to back — one article flows into the next, with optional narrated intros between them that you write yourself.

Line up your morning commute listening. Build a course reading list. Put together a set of essays for your book club. One tap and the whole thing plays with a little chime between documents.

#### Creating a Collection

Go to the Collections tab in your library and tap **New Collection**. It sits at the bottom of the list, or at the end of the grid as a tile with a + sign. You will see a menu with two options: "New Collection" for building one from scratch, and "Create Web Article Collection" for a quick shortcut (more on that below). Choose "New Collection" and give it a name. You can optionally add a description and cover artwork from your photo library, but neither is required.

Then add documents. You will see your full library to pick from. Once they are in, drag to reorder them the way you want, swipe to remove anything that does not belong. The collection detail screen shows an item count, a document count, and a play button right at the top.

You can also add documents to an existing collection without leaving your library. Long-press any document and choose "Add to Collection" from the context menu. Quick and easy.

#### Sorting and Layout

Once you have more than a handful of collections, you will want some control over how they are arranged. The Collections tab has the same two toolbar buttons as your library: sort and layout.

The sort button offers five orders:

- **Recent** — the collections you played most recently come first, then the ones you have never played, newest first. This is the default.
- **Name (A to Z)** — alphabetical.
- **Name (Z to A)** — reverse alphabetical.
- **Newest First** — most recently created collections at the top.
- **Oldest First** — your first collections at the top.

The layout button switches between **List**, **Grid**, and **Compact Grid**, just like in your library. In the grids, each collection shows its cover artwork as a large square, with the name and your progress underneath. A collection without artwork shows its initials on a colored square instead.

Here is the part worth knowing. The Library and Collections tabs remember their layouts separately. You can keep a long document list as a list and still browse your collections as artwork. Change one and the other stays put.

#### Web Article Collections

Here is the scenario. You are importing articles from Safari throughout the day — one here, one there, perhaps five or six by the evening. Now you want to listen through them on a walk. Normally you would create a collection, add each article one by one, name it, and so on. Web Article Collections skip all that.

Tap **New Collection** on the Collections tab and choose "Create Web Article Collection." The app finds all your loose web articles — the ones you have imported but have not organized into a collection yet — and lets you batch them up in two taps. Pick how many you want (5, 10, 25, or all of them), accept or edit the auto-generated name, and tap Create. The collection builds itself from your most recent articles, newest first, and drops you right into it ready to play.

This is the answer to "I want auto-play across documents" and "I want to clean up when I am done." Collections already auto-advance from one document to the next. Once your articles are in a collection, you press play and the whole batch plays straight through. And when you are finished, delete the collection and choose "Delete Collection and Documents" to clear everything out in one shot.

If you do not have any web articles yet, the sheet walks you through how to import them — open Safari, tap share, choose Listen2. And if all your web articles are already in collections, it tells you that too.

#### Intros & Citations

Between any two documents, you can add an intro — a short text segment you write yourself that gets read aloud as a transition. Think of it as the little commentary between tracks on a good radio station. Something like "This next piece is from 1962, when the author was living in Paris..." or even just a sentence of context about what is coming next and why you included it.

Intros show up as expandable items in the collection detail view, each with their own play button. They get narrated by your current voice just like any other text. When you play through a collection, intros play automatically between documents. No extra taps.

For each document in a collection, you can also add source citations — a title and URL pair. Handy for attributing web articles, linking back to the original publication, or giving your reading club members a way to find the source material.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Guided Listening</h4>
<p>Write intros between your documents to create a guided listening experience. Even a sentence or two of context between articles can make a collection feel like a curated podcast.</p>
</div>

#### Playing a Collection

Tap Play on the collection detail screen and it starts from the top. If you have already been listening, it picks up at the first document you have not finished. Progress badges appear on each item: not started, in-progress with a percentage, or done. You always know where you left off.

Want to start the whole collection over? Once you have played a collection, a restart button appears to the left of the Play button. Tap it and confirm, and every document in the collection resets to the beginning. One tap instead of resetting each document individually. Note that this resets each document's actual reading position — so if a document also appears in another collection, its position resets there too.

Inside the reader, the top bar shows the collection name and your position — something like "Morning Reading - 3 of 7." Extra skip buttons appear on either side of the standard controls so you can jump forward or backward between documents in the collection. Skip buttons jump between documents only, not intros. Intros play automatically as transitions when you reach them.

Everything else in the reader works exactly the same. Same speed controls, same highlighting, same playback gestures. The collection just adds that extra layer of structure around it.

#### Sharing & Importing

Tap the export button in the toolbar on a collection's detail screen. Listen2 packages everything up — documents, intros, structure, the works — into a `.listen2collection` file. Share it via AirDrop, Messages, email, or however you prefer. The recipient opens it in Listen2 and gets the whole collection intact, ready to play.

Importing works the other direction. Open a `.listen2collection` file from Files, a link, or another app and Listen2 handles it. If you already have that collection, it lets you know.

#### Deleting a Collection

Swipe left on a collection in the list and tap Delete, or press and hold it (right-click on a Mac) and choose Delete. The grid layouts do not have swipe, so press and hold there. You will get a choice: "Delete Collection Only" or "Delete Collection and Documents."

The first option removes the collection but leaves every document in your library. The structure goes away, but the content stays. The second option removes the collection and deletes the documents along with it — handy when you have finished a batch of articles and want a clean slate.

Here is the safety net: if a document belongs to another collection too, it will not be deleted. Only documents that are exclusively in the collection you are removing get cleaned up. You cannot accidentally lose something that is still part of another playlist.

## 12. Text Display

You are going to be staring at this text for a while — hours, potentially. It should look the way you want it to look — the right font, the right size, the right amount of breathing room between lines. Listen2 gives you real control over all of this, and it is worth spending a minute dialing it in.

#### Fonts

Thirteen fonts across four categories. In the font picker, each font name is rendered in its own typeface, so you can see exactly what you are choosing before you tap.

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "type", "inline-icon" %} Font Categories</h4>
<p><strong>Serif:</strong> Georgia, New York, Palatino, Charter. <strong>Sans-Serif:</strong> System (the default iOS font), Helvetica Neue, Avenir, Avenir Next. <strong>Accessibility:</strong> OpenDyslexic, Atkinson Hyperlegible. <strong>Monospace:</strong> SF Mono, Menlo, Courier.</p>
</div>

Serif fonts tend to feel more like a printed book. Sans-serif fonts feel cleaner and more modern. And the two accessibility fonts are there for a reason — OpenDyslexic is designed to reduce letter-swapping for readers with dyslexia, and Atkinson Hyperlegible maximizes the visual distinction between similar-looking characters for low-vision readers. They are not afterthoughts tucked in a submenu. They are right there in the picker alongside everything else.

#### Size

A slider that runs from 10pt to 40pt. Drag it until the text feels right. But there is also a "Use System Text Size" toggle — flip that on and Listen2 follows whatever you have set in iOS Settings under Display & Text Size, including the larger accessibility sizes. If you have already configured your whole phone for bigger text, Listen2 picks that up automatically. No extra work.

#### Spacing

Two settings here: line spacing and character spacing.

**Line spacing** has five levels — Compact (1.0x, lines right on top of each other), Normal (1.25x, the default), Relaxed (1.5x), Spacious (1.75x), and Very Spacious (2.0x). More space between lines means your eyes have an easier time tracking from the end of one line to the start of the next. That matters more than most people realize, especially during long sessions.

**Character spacing** also has five levels — Default (0%), Slight (5%), Medium (10%), Wide (15%), and Very Wide (20%). This spreads the letters apart within each word. Wider spacing can make a noticeable difference for readability, particularly for readers with dyslexia where tightly packed letters tend to blur together.

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Slight Changes, Significant Impact</h4>
<p>If you find reading tiring, try bumping up line spacing to Relaxed and character spacing to Slight. Slight changes make a significant difference over long reading sessions.</p>
</div>

#### Line Length

On an iPad in landscape, a line of text can stretch all the way across the screen. That is exhausting to read — your eyes have to travel too far from the end of one line to find the start of the next. The Characters Per Line setting constrains the text column width so lines stay a comfortable length.

You get separate sliders for portrait and landscape orientation because what works in one does not always work in the other. As you adjust, a green indicator appears when you are in the 50–75 character range — that is the sweet spot that reading research consistently points to as easiest on the eyes.

This is enabled by default on iPad, where it matters most. On iPhone the screen is already narrow enough that lines do not stretch uncomfortably, so it is off by default. But you can turn it on for either device if you want the control.

## 13. Bookmarks & Search

You are listening to a long essay, and the author drops a paragraph that completely reframes everything you thought about the topic. Or you are working through a textbook and need to flag three definitions across different chapters. Or you just want to jump back to that one scene in chapter twelve. Listen2 gives you three tools for finding your way around a document: bookmarks, search, and the table of contents.

#### Bookmarks

First, the distinction that matters most. Listen2 automatically saves your reading position every time you pause or leave. You never need to do anything to pick up where you left off. Bookmarks are a separate thing entirely — they are for marking specific spots you choose to come back to.

Tap the bookmark button in the reader's top bar to drop a bookmark at your current position. Tap it again to remove it. You can have as many bookmarks as you want in a single document — mark a passage you loved, a key argument, a reference you will need again.

To see all your bookmarks, tap the Table of Contents button in the top bar and switch to the **Bookmarks** tab. Each bookmark shows a snippet of the text at that position and how far through the document it sits. Tap one to jump straight there. Swipe left to delete it, or press and hold it (right-click on a Mac) and choose Delete.

And if you re-import a document or its text changes, Listen2 detects that your bookmarks may have shifted. You will see a warning banner with your options: search manually for the passage using the text snippet shown, or remove the stale bookmark.

Tap the magnifying glass icon in the reader's top bar. Type at least three characters and results appear as a list, each showing the matching text with surrounding context and the chapter or section it belongs to. Tap any result to jump directly to that spot in the document. No complex query syntax, no settings to fiddle with — just type what you are looking for and go.

#### Table of Contents

Tap the Table of Contents button in the reader's top bar. For documents with chapters or sections — EPUBs, structured PDFs — you get a hierarchical list of all chapters and sub-sections. The section you are currently in has a checkmark so you can orient yourself at a glance.

If the document has a lot of entries, a search bar appears at the top to filter the list down. Tap any entry to jump to that chapter.

Note on plain text and clipboard imports: plain text files and clipboard imports will not have a table of contents. There is no structural chapter data to build one from. Bookmarks and search still work fine for those documents.

## 14. iCloud Sync & Backup *(new in v1.9.0)*

A library of documents you have spent time in becomes valuable. Your reading positions, your bookmarks, the voices you have set per book, the pronunciation rules you have written — all of it represents real time spent. Listen2 has two ways to keep that work safe and to move it across devices: iCloud Sync, which keeps multiple devices in agreement automatically, and Backup, which produces a single file you can hold onto for disaster recovery or transfer to a different Apple ID.

Both features are off by default. Both are entirely optional. Both live in the same place: Settings → iCloud & Backup.

#### iCloud Sync

This is the across-devices feature. Turn it on, and any document you import on your iPhone shows up on your Mac and your iPad. Reading positions move with you — pause on one device, pick up on another. Bookmarks, voice presets, pronunciation rules, the whole library state.

Listen2 stores sync data in a private iCloud container. That means the files are not visible to you in the Files app or in iCloud Drive. This is intentional. Sync data is not meant to be browsed or edited by hand — it is the app's internal state, and one accidental swipe could delete reading positions for a hundred documents. The data lives in iCloud, sharing storage quota with your other apps, but the app owns the layout.

**Before you turn it on.** iCloud Sync depends on iCloud Drive being enabled at the device level. iCloud Drive is the system-wide setting that lets apps store data in iCloud at all. If it is off, every app's iCloud storage is unreachable, and Listen2 cannot sync.

To enable iCloud Drive: open the system Settings app, tap your name at the top, tap iCloud, then turn on iCloud Drive. While you are in that screen, scroll down to "Apps Using iCloud" (the exact wording varies by iOS version) and confirm Listen2 is enabled there too. If Listen2 is missing from that list, iCloud Drive itself is probably still off — once you enable it, the app list populates.

If you try to turn on iCloud Sync inside Listen2 without iCloud Drive enabled, you will see an orange warning at the top of the iCloud & Backup section explaining the situation, with an "Open Settings" button. Tap it, and the system Settings app opens to Listen2's app page. Apple does not allow apps to deep-link directly into the iCloud Drive settings panel — you have to back out one level (tap your name at the top), then navigate down to iCloud → iCloud Drive yourself. It is a few extra taps, but the warning makes the path clear.

**Turning it on.** Once iCloud Drive is enabled, open Listen2 → Settings → iCloud & Backup, and toggle iCloud Sync ON. The first time you do this, Listen2 uploads your library, settings, voice presets, and pronunciation rules to your iCloud container. The "Last synced" row updates to show the moment the upload completed, and "Storage used" shows how much of your iCloud quota the data takes.

For an existing user with documents already on the device, the first time you launch a version of Listen2 that supports iCloud Sync, an onboarding sheet appears offering to enable it. Choose "Enable iCloud Sync" and the upload starts in the background. Choose "Not Now" and the sheet does not return — you can always enable it later from Settings.

**How sync moves between devices.** iCloud Sync is eventually consistent, which means changes are propagated through Apple's iCloud infrastructure rather than transmitted device-to-device. Small changes — a position update, a new bookmark — typically appear on the other device within a few seconds. A larger import, like a new EPUB, can take a minute or two depending on iCloud's pace and the file size.

If your destination device's app was backgrounded or freshly launched, it may take a few extra seconds for new content to appear after iCloud has finished propagating. The iOS metadata system that tells Listen2 about new files in iCloud only runs while the app is awake. For now, restarting the app forces a fresh check. A manual refresh button is on the way.

#### Backup: Export

Backup produces a single .listen2backup file — a ZIP archive that contains your entire library. Every document's sidecar (title, position, bookmarks, metadata) plus the source file. Every collection, including its artwork. Your settings. Your voice presets. Your pronunciation rules.

Use it when iCloud Sync is not the right tool. The two big cases: disaster recovery (you are about to delete the app, or factory-reset your phone) and transfer (you want to give your library to a friend's device, or to a second Apple ID you control).

To export, tap "Export Backup" in the iCloud & Backup section. Listen2 offers two modes:

- **Full Backup** includes every document file alongside its metadata. The resulting .listen2backup file is the size of your full library — a few megabytes for plain text, hundreds of megabytes for a library of EPUBs and PDFs. This is the right choice for true disaster recovery. You can restore on any device and have everything back.
- **Metadata Only** includes the sidecars but not the source files. Much smaller — a few hundred kilobytes for most libraries. It restores your reading positions, bookmarks, and library structure, but the actual PDFs and EPUBs would need to be re-imported separately. Use this when the source files live elsewhere (in iCloud Drive, for example) and you only need to preserve the reading state.

After you choose a mode, Listen2 builds the archive and presents the system share sheet. From there, save it to Files (which puts it in iCloud Drive or local storage), email it to yourself, AirDrop it to another device, or whatever else makes sense. The .listen2backup file is portable — it works on any device that has Listen2 installed.

#### Backup: Import

Tap "Import Backup" in the iCloud & Backup section, then pick a .listen2backup file from the file picker. Listen2 reads the archive, shows you what is inside (number of documents and collections, when it was created, which device created it), and asks how to apply it.

Two modes:

- **Merge with My Library** adds the documents from the backup to your existing library. If a document in the backup matches one you already have (same internal ID), Listen2 keeps whichever version was edited most recently. New documents from the backup are added. Existing documents that are not in the backup stay as they are. This is the safe choice — nothing is destroyed.
- **Replace My Library** wipes your existing library completely and restores everything from the backup. Documents you have on the device that are not in the backup are deleted. This cannot be undone. Listen2 confirms before running this mode.

Replace is the right choice when you are intentionally transferring a snapshot from one device to another and want them to match exactly. Merge is the right choice for everything else.

#### When to use which

iCloud Sync and Backup solve overlapping problems with different tradeoffs.

- **iCloud Sync** is for keeping multiple devices in agreement automatically. Turn it on once and forget about it. The cost is that it requires iCloud Drive to be enabled, and changes can take a moment to propagate.
- **Backup** is for moments when you want a single file you control. Disaster recovery, transfer to a different Apple ID, or just a snapshot you stash somewhere safe. A Full Backup is independent of iCloud — it works even with iCloud Sync turned off entirely.

A useful pattern: use iCloud Sync as your day-to-day mechanism for keeping devices in agreement, and export a Full Backup occasionally as an extra safety net.

#### A caveat about deleting the app

When you delete Listen2 from your device, iOS asks whether to keep or delete the app's iCloud data. This dialog is easy to miss and easy to misread. The choice matters: if you tap "Delete," your iCloud container is wiped, and your synced library is gone. There is no undo, no restore from Apple. The data is genuinely deleted from iCloud.

If you are deleting the app because you plan to reinstall it (testing a beta, troubleshooting, getting a new phone), always choose "Keep" when iOS asks about iCloud data. After reinstalling, turn iCloud Sync back on and your library will repopulate.

If you choose "Delete" by accident, your only recovery path is a Backup file you exported beforehand. This is one of the strongest reasons to take a Full Backup occasionally — it is the only safety net for that scenario.

## 15. Accessibility

A text-to-speech app is, by definition, an accessibility tool. It takes text and turns it into audio. Accessibility here is not a feature that was tacked on after the fact — it is the whole premise. Every screen, every button, every interaction was designed to work with VoiceOver and other assistive technologies from the start.

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "accessibility", "inline-icon" %} Accessibility Features at a Glance</h4>
<p>Full VoiceOver support on every screen. Five levels of text navigation — lines, sentences, paragraphs, words, and characters — all accessible through the VoiceOver rotor. Bookmark and copy actions available directly from the reading text via the Actions rotor. Two accessible fonts — OpenDyslexic and Atkinson Hyperlegible. Dynamic Type integration with all iOS accessibility size categories. Generous line spacing up to 2.0x and character spacing up to 20%. Seekable progress bar with adjustable actions for VoiceOver (5% increments). Timer controls with descriptive labels, values, and hints. Engine badges on voice rows that read as "{engine} engine." AI-generated figure descriptions that make images in your documents audible. A voice-creation flow that never speaks while the microphone is listening, with haptics and tones in place of spoken prompts and a "speak in your own words" option instead of reading a script.</p>
</div>

#### VoiceOver

Every button has a descriptive label and hint. Interactive elements use appropriate traits — adjustable for sliders, selected for active states, header for section titles. None of this is generic placeholder text. Each label tells you what the control does, and each hint tells you what happens when you use it.

Rotor actions are available throughout the app. In the Voice Library, Play Sample and Stop Sample are rotor actions. In lists, delete is a rotor action. In the reader, copy and bookmark are rotor actions on the text itself.

The reader provides five navigation levels through the VoiceOver rotor: lines (which flow across paragraph boundaries with auto-scroll), sentences, paragraphs, words, and characters. The Actions rotor on the reading text gives you bookmark, copy (line, sentence, or paragraph), and play-from-here. See the Reader Controls callout in section 4 for the full list.

When VoiceOver is active, the top and bottom bars stay visible at all times. Auto-scrolling is disabled so the text does not move under your focus. The "Jump to Playback Position" button is available in the Navigate sheet (tap the Navigate button in the bottom bar) and brings the current reading line back into view.

Two-finger double-tap (Magic Tap) toggles play and pause. Two-finger Z scrub dismisses back to the library. Two-finger swipe down (Read All) reads through the entire document with automatic content loading at section boundaries.

Each earlier section of this guide includes a VoiceOver callout covering that specific screen's interactions. If you are looking for how VoiceOver works on a particular screen, check that section first.

#### Accessible Fonts

Two fonts designed specifically for readability, right alongside every other font in the picker:

**OpenDyslexic** uses weighted letterforms — heavier at the bottom — to reduce the letter-swapping and rotation that can happen for readers with dyslexia. Letters stay anchored on the page.

**Atkinson Hyperlegible** was designed by the Braille Institute to maximize the visual distinction between similar characters. The difference between l, 1, and I is obvious. Same for 0 and O. Built for low-vision readers, but plenty of people find it easier to read in general.

#### Dynamic Type

Toggle "Use System Text Size" in text display settings and Listen2 follows your iOS text size preference. That includes all the larger accessibility size categories. Whatever you have configured system-wide carries straight through — no extra setup.

#### Spacing

Line spacing goes up to 2.0x and character spacing up to 20%. Both meet or exceed WCAG guidelines for text spacing. But you do not need an accessibility reason to use them. Wider spacing reduces eye strain during long reading sessions for everyone. Sliders support VoiceOver adjustable actions — swipe up to increase, swipe down to decrease.

## 16. Tips, Troubleshooting & Support

Now that you have explored everything the app can do, here is the quick-reference card — practical advice, common fixes, and where to go if something does not work right.

#### Best Practices

<div class="tip-box">
<h4>{% icon "lightbulb", "inline-icon" %} Getting Started Right</h4>
<p>Download voices over Wi-Fi — they are 60–75 MB each. Use headphones for the best audio quality. And start at 1.0× speed, then work up gradually. Most people land around 1.2×–1.5× for casual content.</p>

If word-level highlighting feels too busy, switch to sentence highlighting. You still get a visual anchor tracking your position, but it is calmer. Less movement on the screen.

On iPad, check the line length setting. Without it, text stretches edge to edge on a 12.9-inch screen and your eyes have to travel a long distance to reach the next line. Constraining the width makes a real difference for longer reading sessions.

#### Trial & Purchase

<div class="feature-box">
<h4>{% icon "tag", "inline-icon" %} One-Time Purchase</h4>
<p>Listen2 has a 7-day free trial with full access to everything — no restrictions, no feature gates. After that, it is a one-time purchase of $24.99. Not a subscription. You buy it once and it is yours. No account needed. No sign-up. No data collection.</p>

You do not have to wait for the trial to end. Go to Settings and tap "Upgrade." Listen2 shows you what is included and the price, and you can buy it right there. When the trial does end, the same screen appears the first time you press play.

If you reinstall the app or switch to a new device, go to Settings and tap "Restore Purchases." Your purchase is tied to your Apple Account, so it comes right back. No receipt to dig up, no code to enter.

If a restore does not work, Listen2 tells you why. The most common reason is that the device is not signed in to the App Store with the Apple Account you bought Listen2 with. iCloud and App Store purchases are separate sign-ins on iPhone and iPad, so you can be signed in to one and not the other. Go to Settings, tap your name, then tap Media & Purchases. If it says "Sign In," or shows a different email than the one you bought with, sign in with the right account. Then tap "Restore Purchases" again. That is it.

#### Common Issues

**Listen2 asks you to buy it again.** This happens when the device is not signed in to the App Store with the Apple Account you bought Listen2 with. A telltale sign: you tap "Restore Purchase," and the Apple sign-in box appears with the email field empty. Go to Settings, tap your name, then tap Media & Purchases, and sign in with the account you bought with. Then tap "Restore Purchase" in Listen2.

**Voice sounds choppy or delayed.** Close other apps to free up memory. If you are using word-level highlighting, try switching to sentence-level — it uses less processing power. Restarting the app can help too. On older devices, this makes the biggest difference.

**Document will not import.** First, check the format. Listen2 supports PDF, EPUB, DOCX, TXT, MD, and DAISY. If a format should work but is not loading, try a different import method — "Import from Link" instead of the Share Extension, or vice versa.

<div class="warning-box">
<h4>{% icon "alert-triangle", "inline-icon" %} Scanned PDFs</h4>
<p>If a PDF imports but shows garbled text or nothing at all, it is likely a scanned image rather than real text. Listen2 cannot read pixels — it needs actual text content. Try copying the text manually from the source and using clipboard import instead.</p>
</div>

**Supertonic voice switches between accents.** You might notice a Supertonic voice drifting between British and American accents within the same document. This is a known characteristic of the underlying model — the voice learned from a mix of accent styles, and certain text patterns can push it toward one or the other. Lowering the Variation slider in the Voice Profile can help stabilize the accent. This is something we are actively working to improve.

**Kokoro voices not showing up.** Kokoro requires a device with at least 6 GB of RAM. On older iPhones and iPads that do not meet this threshold, Kokoro voices will not appear in the Voice Library. This is not a bug — the model is too large to run reliably on those devices.

**Voice will not delete.** Two common causes. First: the voice is in use. You cannot delete the active voice — and because Supertonic and Kokoro voices share one engine, you cannot delete any voice from those families while one of them is active. Switch to a different voice, then delete. Second: it is an Apple system voice. Those belong to iOS, not Listen 2, so the app cannot remove them. Manage them in the Settings app under Accessibility, then Spoken Content, then Voices.

**Playback paused while my phone was locked.** iOS limits how much processing an app can use while your phone is locked, and the voice you were using needed more than that. Listen2 paused at the end of a sentence instead of being shut down by iOS. Unlock your phone and press play to continue from the next sentence. If this happens often, switch to a Phonon or Supertonic voice for locked-screen listening. See "When Your Phone Is Locked" in the Playback section.

**Phone gets warm with Kokoro.** Because Kokoro runs a larger neural network on device, your phone will produce more heat and use more energy during extended playback compared to Piper. This is normal. If heat or battery life is a concern for a particular session, switch to a Piper voice — or for the absolute lowest energy impact, use an Apple Premium or Enhanced system voice.

**Highlighting seems out of sync.** This can happen with unusual formatting or at very fast speech rates. The audio itself is still correct — it is only the visual tracking that may drift. Switching to sentence-level highlighting smooths it out.

**Voice keeps mispronouncing a word.** Create a pronunciation rule. Tap the Audio button (speaker icon) in the bottom bar while reading, tap Pronunciation Rules, and add a rule for that word. With a Listen2 voice, choose **Sounds Like** and write how the word sounds, with hyphens between the parts and the loudest part in capitals: SEER-shuh. With other voices, a **Replace** rule works best: write the replacement as a natural word the voice can read. For abbreviations, use periods between letters (N.A.S.A.) instead of spaces. See the Pronunciation section for both.

**A Listen2 voice says "read" or "lead" the wrong way.** Check that **Read Words by Their Meaning** is on in the Pronunciation section of Settings. When Listen2 is not sure which reading a sentence needs, it leaves the word to the voice, so an unusual sentence can still come out wrong. A Sounds Like rule scoped to that document fixes it for good.

**The sentence starts over when I press play.** That is expected after a pause of more than eight seconds. Listen2 releases your speaker or headphones while paused, and starts the current sentence again when you come back. See "After a Long Pause" in the Playback section.

**Words are highlighting in the car, but there is no sound.** Check that the car is set to Bluetooth audio and that its volume is up. Then press play again. If it is still silent, switch the output to your iPhone in Control Center for a moment. If you hear Listen2 there, the problem is the connection to the car. See "Car, Bluetooth and Headphone Controls" in the Playback section, and if it keeps happening, contact support with your car's make and model.

**A voice I used is missing from the Voice Library.** Some older voices were retired in version 2.0. If you had downloaded one, it is still on your device, marked Legacy and "No longer offered." If you had not, it can no longer be downloaded, but a retrained 1.5 version or a similar new voice may be there. See "Voices No Longer Offered" in the Voices & Languages section.

**Creating a voice: the two recordings did not match.** The consent check compares the person who read the passage with the person who said the sentence. A change of room, distance or microphone between the two can make the same person sound different. Say the sentence again from the same spot, with the same microphone. If it still fails, start over and record both parts in one sitting.

**Creating a voice: "That was a little short."** A voice needs at least five seconds of steady speech. Read the whole passage, which takes about twenty seconds, and keep going through the commas instead of pausing at each one.

**Non-English voice mispronouncing numbers or dates.** Download the language pack for that voice's language. Go to the Voice Library, then Language Packs. These grammar packs teach the voice how to expand numbers, dates, and currency into proper spoken words. English has one built in, but other languages need the download.

**Missing Table of Contents.** Not all documents have chapter structure. Plain text files, clipboard imports, and some PDFs will not have a TOC. EPUBs almost always do. This is not a bug — there is simply no chapter data to work with.

**iCloud Sync toggle does nothing, or shows a warning.** The most common cause is iCloud Drive being off at the device level. Open the system Settings app, tap your name, tap iCloud, and confirm iCloud Drive is on. Then scroll down to "Apps Using iCloud" and confirm Listen2 is enabled there too. The iCloud & Backup section in Listen2 will surface a warning when this is the situation, with an "Open Settings" button that takes you to Listen2's app page — back out one level from there to reach iCloud.

**Documents synced on one device do not appear on another.** Give iCloud a few minutes to propagate, especially for larger imports. If the destination device's app was backgrounded or just launched, relaunching it forces a fresh check against iCloud. If documents still do not appear after several minutes, confirm both devices are signed into the same iCloud account and that iCloud Sync is on in Listen2 on each.

**Reinstalled the app and the library is empty.** When deleting the app, iOS asks whether to keep or delete the iCloud data — if you tapped "Delete," your synced library was wiped from iCloud and cannot be recovered through sync. The only path back is a Backup file you exported beforehand. Going forward, always choose "Keep" when iOS asks during deletion, and consider exporting a Full Backup occasionally as an extra safety net.
